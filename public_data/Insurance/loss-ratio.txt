LOSS RATIO & UNDERWRITING METRICS
================================

The Loss Ratio is the foundational financial and operational metric in the insurance industry. It assesses the underwriting profitability and risk-selection quality of an insurance company by comparing the costs of claims incurred against the revenues generated from policy premiums.

CONCEPT OVERVIEW
----------------
An insurance company's core operational function is to accept premium from policyholders in exchange for the promise of indemnifying them in the event of covered future losses. The loss ratio quantifies how much of that premium is paid out to resolve those losses. It is calculated as incurred losses divided by earned premium, expressed as a percentage:

Loss Ratio = (Incurred Losses / Earned Premium) * 100

INCURRED LOSSES BREAKDOWN
-------------------------
Incurred losses are not simply a tally of checks written during the year. True "Incurred Losses" represent the total estimated liabilities arising from events that occurred during the policy period, consisting of three distinct financial components:

Incurred Losses = Paid Claims + Case Reserves + IBNR (Incurred But Not Reported) Reserves

1. Paid Claims: The actual cash amounts paid to claimants to settle claims.
2. Case Reserves: Funds set aside by claims adjusters for individual known claims that are still open and pending resolution.
3. IBNR (Incurred But Not Reported): Actuarial estimations of liabilities for claims that have already occurred but have not yet been reported to the insurance carrier, as well as adjustments to case reserves.

REVENUE: WRITTEN VS EARNED PREMIUM
----------------------------------
One of the most frequent errors in insurance analysis is confusing Written Premium with Earned Premium:
- Written Premium: The total premium charged for all policies written during a specific period. If an insurer writes a 12-month policy on July 1 for $1,200, the written premium is immediately $1,200.
- Earned Premium: The portion of written premium that corresponds to the elapsed portion of the policy term. Premium "earns out" on a daily pro-rata basis as the insurer fulfills its promise of coverage over time.

By December 31 (6 months later), the insurer has fulfilled half of its coverage promise, making the Earned Premium $600. The remaining $600 is classified on the balance sheet as Unearned Premium Reserve (UPR) and cannot be recognized as revenue yet.

THE COMBINED RATIO
------------------
To evaluate the absolute underwriting profitability of an insurance company, the Loss Ratio must be combined with the Expense Ratio, yielding the Combined Ratio:

Combined Ratio = Loss Ratio + Expense Ratio

- Expense Ratio: The percentage of premium spent on operating expenses (e.g., agent commissions, underwriting staff salaries, marketing, legal compliance, and technology platforms).
- Interpretation:
  - Combined Ratio < 100%: Underwriting Profitability. The insurer made money on its insurance operations alone.
  - Combined Ratio > 100%: Underwriting Loss. The insurer spent more on claims and operations than it collected in premiums, relying on investment income from premium float to generate net profit.

REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY
---------------------
Let's analyze an insurer with the following annual figures:
- Gross Written Premium: $50,000,000
- Earned Premium: $40,000,000
- Paid Claims: $18,000,000
- Ending Case Reserves: $4,000,000
- IBNR Actuarial Adjustments: $2,000,000
- Operational & Commission Expenses: $14,000,000

Calculations:
1. Total Incurred Losses = $18,000,000 + $4,000,000 + $2,000,000 = $24,000,000
2. Loss Ratio = ($24,000,000 / $40,000,000) * 100 = 60.0%
3. Expense Ratio = ($14,000,000 / $40,000,000) * 100 = 35.0%
4. Combined Ratio = 60.0% + 35.0% = 95.0% (Underwriting profit of 5.0%)

COMMON MISTAKES
---------------
1. Dividing Incurred Losses by Written Premium: This mismatch understates the true loss ratio for growing insurers because written premium is recognized instantly while losses correspond to earned premium.
2. Ignoring Development Drift: Assuming that case reserves are fixed. Under-reserving leads to "adverse loss development" in future years, artificially deflating the current year's loss ratio.
3. Equating Loss Ratio with Profitability: Insurers with high investment yields (e.g. from high-interest bonds) can remain highly profitable overall even with combined ratios slightly above 100%.

KEY TAKEAWAYS
-------------
- The Loss Ratio compares incurred losses (claims paid + reserves) to earned premium (accrued revenue), reflecting pure risk-selection performance.
- Earned premium matches revenue to the period of active exposure, whereas written premium represents future contract value.
- A combined ratio under 100% is the gold standard for underwriting operational discipline.
